\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

When a person watches a machine make a mistake, or makes one themselves, the brain produces an
error-related potential (ErrP): a fronto-central negativity followed by a positivity within a few hundred
milliseconds of the error~\citep{chavarriaga2014errare}. Because ErrPs arise without any explicit action,
they have long been proposed as an \emph{implicit} reward signal for teaching robots and
interfaces~\citep{iturrate2015teaching,kim2017intrinsic,salazargomez2017correcting,xu2021accelerating}.
Two developments make this question timely. First, EEG \emph{foundation models} pretrained on thousands of
hours of recordings~\citep{jiang2024labram,wang2024eegpt,wang2025cbramod} promise decoders that transfer
to new people without calibration. Second, learning from human feedback has become a central tool in
machine learning~\citep{christiano2017deep}, and a brain signal would make such feedback free.

Whether either promise holds for ErrPs is unclear. Existing EEG foundation-model benchmarks cover many
paradigms but at most one ErrP dataset~\citep{lu2026omnieegbench,liu2026eegfmcompass}, and ErrP studies typically
evaluate a single dataset with its own protocol. On the RL side, studies of ErrP-based reward report
gains from simulated or near-ideal feedback, but rarely ask how good a \emph{real} cross-subject decoder must
be before its feedback helps.

We address both questions with a method, a benchmark and a measurement:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{ErrP-Ens}, a calibration-free decoder (\S\ref{sec:method}). Each participant's EEG is aligned
  without labels; decoders of three families are trained on other participants with and without alignment; and
  their outputs are combined by averaging within-participant ranks, which is invariant to the scale and offset
  differences that make score averaging fail across models and people. ErrP-Ens needs neither training nor
  labels from the new user.
\item \textbf{ErrP-Bench}, five public ErrP datasets across four error paradigms: observed robot errors and
  observed cursor errors~\citep{ehrlich2019feasibility}, cursor-monitoring errors~\citep{chavarriaga2010learning},
  self-generated response errors (ERN, flanker task)~\citep{kappenman2021erpcore}, and errors introduced by an
  arm orthosis~\citep{kueper2024eegemg}; \NSubjectsTotal{} participants and \NEpochsTotal{} epochs, all used in
  full under one preprocessing and cross-subject protocol, with a Riemannian pipeline~\citep{rivet2009xdawn,barachant2012multiclass},
  five compact networks~\citep{lawhern2018eegnet,santamariavazquez2020eeginception,chen2024eegnex,song2023eegconformer,zhao2024ctnet}
  and three foundation models~\citep{jiang2024labram,wang2024eegpt,wang2025cbramod}, each with and without
  Euclidean alignment~\citep{he2020transfer}; \NFitsTotal{} cross-subject fits.
\item \textbf{A decoder-quality frontier for learning from ErrP feedback}: soft actor-critic~\citep{haarnoja2018sac}
  on FetchReach and FetchPush~\citep{plappert2018multigoal} with feedback of exactly controlled AUROC, which
  locates the break-even decoder quality at which ErrP reward starts to help, and places every benchmark decoder
  relative to it.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Findings.} (1) ErrP-Ens is the best cross-subject ErrP decoder on all five datasets and is never worse
than the best single decoder chosen with hindsight. (2) Pretrained EEG foundation models do not beat compact
networks on cross-subject ErrP detection. (3) The observed agent matters more than the decoder: the same
participants give \BestErrpHriCursor{} AUROC for cursor errors and \BestErrpHriRobot{} for robot errors.
(4) Unsupervised alignment helps on some paradigms and hurts on others, which is why ErrP-Ens uses both.
(5) On simulated reaching, ErrP feedback helps only above about AUROC \RLBreakEvenReach{} and slows learning
below it, so cross-subject decoders of observed robot errors are not yet on the useful side of the frontier.

All code, data-preparation recipes, per-trial predictions and checkpoints are released.
